# Evidence discipline for issues, PRs, and comments

Whether a claim is **true**, as distinct from how it is written — that is `PROSE-DISCIPLINE.md`'s job, and these two were one file until 2026-09-04, when the evidence half had grown to 48% of a document titled for style.

**Apply these whenever you cite a number or write a correction** — drafting a body is the common case, not the trigger. A figure quoted in a commit message, a cross-session message, or a doc comment needs the same checks and gets them less often.

**Budget.** This is the half that grows: ten consecutive kaizen commits to this repo added 166 lines and deleted 7, and nearly all of it landed here. Prefer a change that is roughly line-neutral — a new rule can usually pay for itself out of the previous rule's justification prose. Keep the clause that makes a measured example undeniable; cut the parameter-space texture around it.

## Before citing a measurement

A number in an issue body licenses work. Before one goes in, establish what its presence in a file does not establish on its own.

- **Is it derived from the data, or from the configuration?** A field can vary across runs, carry a plausible name, and still be a restatement of the flags. Measured on `matsengrp/phyz` 2026-09-02: `--summary-tsv`'s `search_rounds` read exactly 42 in all 43 cells of a sweep, because it resolved to `max_failures + free_pool_slots` — inputs the operator had chosen. It looked like search depth and was an echo of the command line. The cheap tell is to predict the value from the flags alone before opening the file; if you can, it is not evidence. A field that varies with `num_leaves` is the harder case, because tracking one input looks more like measurement than tracking none.
- **Does it reach disk, where a reader can open it?** Four separate times in one day the answer was no, in four different ways: a cited `results/raw/` path was gitignored and absent from a fresh clone; the stderr files holding an experiment's primary evidence matched no `*.tsv` negation at any depth; a harness discarded the subprocess stdout carrying the column it was there to collect; and a rescued copy lived at a path nothing committed referenced. Each was invisible in the same way — the run produced the quantity, and nothing that survived the run recorded it. **Sender-side: when you hand a figure to someone who may cite it, state its path — or state that it has none.** Three of one night's retracted numbers crossed a session boundary first; the relay is where a claim acquires the relayer's authority without acquiring any of its evidence.
- **A rule that exists and is not applied is a different problem from a missing one, and it is the one this file has.** Measured on `matsengrp/phyz` 2026-09-12: repeatedly in one night, a check returned a clean negative without having read its input, several of them reaching or nearly reaching a verdict — and **every one had a written rule in this file sitting unapplied behind it** — the grep-the-claim's-subject rule below, and "a detector's negative is not evidence until the detector is shown to fire." A fourth phrasing of either would have been the least useful response available. **Before adding a rule here, check whether the one you need is already present and simply did not fire.** The single mechanism from that night genuinely absent here is also the worked example for why non-application is the norm rather than the exception: **a sign convention lives in a metric's *definition*, while the sentence interpreting it is written from the *values*, so every check that compares figures passes while the prose inverts.** A `defect_count` (lower is better) was defined with that convention stated explicitly, inverted in two sentences of one commit, passed two independent gate reviews — and was then written backwards a **third** time into the very sentence repairing the first two, by an author who had the convention in mind and had been corrected on it minutes earlier. Nobody misremembered it. **State the convention where you cite the number, not only where you define it.**

- **Is it a census, or the items you already had?** Re-derivation validates the members of a set and cannot find a missing one — every item checks out and the count is still wrong. For any "which items have property P" claim, derive the population from P rather than from familiarity, then evaluate P on every member: if P is "merged before commit X," the population is every merge, not the issues you can name. **Listing the population is not checking it** — a complete list with a partial inspection still yields an overstated count. Re-checking the items you already had is a different operation again, not a more careful version of either.

- **Is it the whole result, or the part that arrived first?** A partial result licenses no direction, and hedging it does not fix that. Measured 2026-09-02: the first band of a four-band experiment showed a lever helping where prior work said it hurt; two sessions independently hedged and then stated the direction anyway. It reversed on the full table. **The hedge travelled less far than the direction did.** Report the partial number if it is useful; do not attach a reading to it, because the reading is what gets quoted.

The two failures compose, and the composition is the one to watch for: a number that is a configuration artifact **and** unreadable by the next person reads exactly like a hard-won result, because nobody can check it. Verify the capture path end to end on one case before running anything at scale, and quote the command that produced the number rather than the file it landed in.

This is the evidence-side twin of the guard rule above: there, a check that cannot read its input reports a safety it never established; here, a citation that cannot be opened reports an observation nobody made.

## Name the question your check answers, not the one you asked

**A check that runs, succeeds, and answers an adjacent question is more dangerous than one that fails**, because it is reported as having settled the thing it did not settle. Most instances below are from `matsengrp/phyz` (2026-09-06 and 2026-09-11) and read as a clean result at the time. **Two fire the other way, as a false ALARM**, and the second of those is worse than any wrong zero here: **an alarm that mimics the exact failure you are hunting prompts action rather than closing a question**, so it costs a wrong conclusion rather than a missed one.

- **A tool answering about your query rather than the world.** `grep -c` on a log returned 0 and looked like data loss; the source read `` `pcp-pipeline` `` with backticks and the pattern had none. Same shape, 2026-09-11: `git ls-files` reported an artifact uncommitted while the working tree sat four commits behind `origin/main` — it answers *"is it in my index,"* not *"is it committed"* (`git ls-tree -r origin/main` answers the latter). A whole issue draft was written on the resulting false premise. **A zero, or an absence, is a statement about your pattern and your view before it is a statement about the data.** Third costume, same day: a harness reported a background build as `exit code 0` when the build had failed — the `0` was a trailing `echo`'s status. **A reported exit code describes the last command in the chain, not the one you cared about.** The conductor session caught it only because it had captured the build's own status into a named variable; do that rather than reading the chain's. **Fourth costume, and this one is environmental rather than a pattern slip: `grep` may not be `grep`.** Measured 2026-09-12 on `pax`: in a Claude Code session `grep` is a shell *function* from `~/.claude/shell-snapshots/snapshot-zsh-*.sh` routing to `ugrep 7.8.4`, and ugrep treats a **mid-pattern `$` as an anchor** where POSIX BRE and GNU grep 3.11 treat it as literal. So `grep -c 'print $NF'` returns **0** against a file containing exactly `print $NF`, while `/usr/bin/grep -c` on the same file returns 1. The pattern is not merely non-matching, it is **unsatisfiable** — 0 against every possible input. **And it corrupts the zero-based claim specifically, which is the asymmetry that makes it dangerous: a wrong non-zero count is visible on inspection, while a wrong zero reads as "verified absent" and closes the question.** Two sessions hit this within ten minutes the same hour, each searching for *shell code* (`$bc`, `$(`, `$NF`), each within one step of reporting a file's contents absent; it read as a shared human slip and was a shared tool. **Fleet tooling is made of `$`, so this fires precisely where it is most load-bearing.** Call `/usr/bin/grep` explicitly for anything with alternation, and reserve `grep -F` for a **single literal pattern** — `-F` and alternation are mutually exclusive, see the next bullet — and when a search for something you expect returns zero, `type grep` is the one-command check that the tool is the one you think.
- **Fifth costume, and it is the remedy prescribed in the bullet above.** **Alternation → `/usr/bin/grep -E`; `-F` → exactly one pattern per invocation; never read a zero from a multi-pattern `-F` probe as evidence of absence.** In fixed-string mode `\|` is a *literal backslash-pipe*, not alternation, so the probe searches for one string that does not exist and returns **0 hits, exit 1, no error**. Measured 2026-09-13 on `matsengrp/phyz`: `grep -F 'regenerate\|historical record\|re-run' experiments/CLAUDE.md` exits 1 with no output, while `/usr/bin/grep -c -E` on the same three patterns returns **3** (lines 375, 389, 624). The zero was cited as evidence that a file did not already cover a rule, in an issue proposing to add that rule — the file carried it in two places. **This correction's own remedy has not been stress-tested either**: `/usr/bin/grep -E` is right for alternation, and nobody has yet asked what *its* adjacent failure is. Writing that down is the only thing that stops this shape recurring a third time under a third guard.

- **A state observed DURING a transition is not a state — and here the instrument is right, only the timing is wrong.** The other entries in this section are checks pointed at an adjacent *question*; this one is the correct check at an adjacent *instant*, which is why "search harder" does not reach it. Three instances on `matsengrp/phyz` 2026-09-12, all inside one hour: a process reported `STILL ALIVE` that was already exiting; a `claude` process sampled at `ppid=1` two seconds into teardown, yielding the confident conclusion *"`tmux kill-window` orphans the process"* — it was gone moments later; and a `find` over a preservation directory that correctly reported an artifact absent **47 seconds before the ceremony that creates it wrote it**, yielding *"the land-time preservation step did not run."* Each search was accurate at the instant it ran, and each conclusion was false. **Before concluding anything about a system that is visibly mid-operation, sample twice.** The tell is cheap: a worker that has just merged a PR, a window you just closed, a process you just signalled — all are mid-operation by construction, and **elapsed time is what separates a transient from a steady state** (a two-day-old orphan is real; a two-second one is teardown). **And a plausible self-diagnosis that fits a known failure shape is still only a hypothesis** — the `find` above was first blamed on its own `| head -8` truncation, a real failure mode named in this very file; checking showed the match sat at line 2 of the output, well inside the limit, and the truncation was innocent. **"Sample twice" is not enough on its own, because it does not say WHEN — and the gap that kills you is inside your own turn.** Measured 2026-09-13 on `matsengrp/phyz`: a session read `state=OPEN` from `gh pr view`, ran **two further commands**, then posted a blocking comment — which landed **30 seconds after the PR merged**, advising a hold on something already done. The read was correct when it ran. **It had also been told, in as many words, that the PR was being landed right then**, so this bullet's own tell was in hand and did not fire — *a state read is timestamped, and it decays between observing and acting even when nothing warns you.* ➡ **For anything outward-facing, re-check the state in the command that acts, not in a command before it.** That is mechanical and testable, where "be careful" and "sample twice" are neither. The same session had internalised *"don't inherit fleet state from a message"* and applied it to every peer all day; **the form that catches this is "don't inherit it from yourself either, across intervening tool calls."** **Both authors of this remedy then broke it, independently, the same day, hours after committing it** — one reporting a PR as open that had merged, one reporting a worklog as unpreserved that had just been preserved. That is not two slips: **a rule about your own sampling instant has no external trigger**, so it gets enforced outward and missed inward. ➡ **The tell is that you are about to report a state you observed rather than one you are observing.**
- **A filename match that was a comment.** `grep -rl converge_rung` hit a file that only *mentioned* the script in prose. Twice more the same day, on unrelated code. **The filename is not the signal; the call is.**
- **The check whose reassuring output is its own failure signal** (2026-09-11). Certifying that a rebase preserved reviewed content, `git diff <old-head> <new-head>` was reported as empty and read as "content unchanged." It was not empty — and an empty result there would have meant *the rebase never happened*, since the tree must gain whatever the new base added. Across a rebase the tree is **supposed** to change, so only patch-vs-patch (`git diff <base>...<head>` on each side, then diff the two patches) isolates your own change, paired with `git merge-base --is-ancestor` to distinguish a rebase from a re-point. **When a check's pass condition is indistinguishable from its failure condition, it is not a check** — and this one was being used to certify a gate.
- ⛔ **A NULL reading whose instrument had no reachable NON-NULL branch in that run.** ⭐ **The sharper, more actionable special case of the bullet above** — it names *which* branch was unreachable and therefore *what* positive control to run. Do not spend effort deciding which of the two a case belongs to; the remedy is the same. **Three instances on `matsengrp/phyz` 2026-09-19, in three unrelated subsystems, within one morning:** a counter **declared, zeroed, and never incremented** — IQ-TREE's `num_partial_lh_computations`, both increment sites unreachable (`tree/phylokernel.h:88` sits inside the block comment opened at `:78` and closed at `:1395`, and that file **is** `#include`d by four kernel TUs, which is what makes it easy to misread: live include, dead code; `phylokernelsafe.h` is never included), where the issue's own instruction *"uncomment the print"* yields `Total number of partial likelihood vector computations: 0`, **a precise, plausible, confident zero from an instrument that was never wired**; a **guard that could not fire** — `scripts/check_remote_sync_excludes.sh:39` tests a captured `make` stdout for emptiness, and with `w` in `MAKEFLAGS` (exported by any sub-make) the `Entering directory` lines land in the variable, so `[ -z ]` is never true and execution falls through to a later assertion failing for an unrelated reason, **exit 2 with a specific reproducible message and nothing about the guard ever decided**; and a **search that was killed** — a `find` over an NFS tree wrapped in `timeout 120`, killed mid-walk, emitting nothing, pipeline exiting 0, a clean "not found" for a file that was there the whole time. **In each case the reader assumed a two-outcome instrument where only one outcome was ever available**: the increment was not compiled in, the true branch could not be entered, the walk was cut off before it could reach a match. ➡ **Observe the instrument produce a NON-NULL result before trusting a NULL one** — a counter you have never seen non-zero, a guard you have never seen fire, a search you have never seen match, none is evidence of absence. **For a regression test the operative form is stronger: run it against the unfixed code and see it FAIL before it counts.** A test written after a fix and never seen red is the same object as the guard that could not fire. ⚠ **What makes this class survive review is that each reading is a legitimate value** — zero is a real count, empty is a real result, not-found is a real answer — so nothing is out of range and nothing prompts a second look, which is why "be careful" does not reach it and a positive control does. ⚠ **This is a unification and is confident across these three and no further**: the shared property to check against a fourth instance is *the non-null branch was unreachable in that run*, not any shared mechanism — the three fixes land in three different subsystems, and only the reading error and its remedy are common.
  - ⛔ **THE VERIFICATION COSTUME OF THE BULLET ABOVE: A SECOND PARTY USING THE FIRST PARTY'S SPECIFICATION IS A SPELL-CHECKER, NOT A CHECK.** The null reading is *"no discrepancy found"*; the non-null branch is *"a discrepancy"*; and **sharing the specification is what makes that branch unreachable** — if both parties compute under the same spec, a spec error *cannot* produce disagreement. ⚠ **This needs no instances to establish: it is true before anyone does it.** Measured on `matsengrp/phyz` 2026-09-19 anyway, because the shape is worth seeing: one party published a per-branch-per-sweep ratio under the wrong `n` formula **and** divided one engine's counts by the *other* engine's sweep column; a second party re-derived, matched to the last digit, and reported exact agreement as corroboration, in messages whose stated purpose was verification. The published conclusion (*"IQ-TREE is falling, not flat"*) was the opposite of the truth and was used to tell an issue's implementer not to trust its own correct figures. ⭐ **Exact agreement is the strongest available signal of correctness, which is precisely why no amount of attention separates this from a real check.** ➡ **The remedy is a different ACTION, not more care: RE-DERIVE FROM THE SOURCE THE CLAIM CITES, NOT FROM THE CLAIM.** ⚠ **And the tell is usually free.** In the case above the issue body named its denominator the entire time; **both parties re-derived and neither opened the one artifact that specified what to re-derive.** ⭐ **The correct figures were available to both from the artifact under review, and the wrong ones took more work to produce** — re-deriving is effortful and reads as diligence, while opening the cited file is trivial and reads as not bothering. **The failure has a cost gradient pointing the wrong way, which is why "be more rigorous" makes it worse rather than better.** ➡ **When a claim cites a file, a formula or a column, read that — a recomputation under the claimant's spec tests arithmetic and cannot test a spec.**
- ⛔ **A TABLE'S COLUMN NAMES DO NOT SPECIFY THE MODEL THAT COMBINES THEM, and a re-derivation under the wrong model produces a plausible number from a correctly-named source.** Distinct from the population and denominator entries in this file: here the population is right, every column is the one the claim names, and the arithmetic is correct — **what differs is the ESTIMATOR.** Two instances on `matsengrp/phyz` 2026-09-19, one morning, one artifact. **Aggregation order:** a published ratio was `median(A)/median(B)` where the sibling table's stated convention was the median of per-start ratios. Same two columns. At the primary cell, 5.38 against 6.24 — and **4 of 6 fixtures were pairing a numerator from one run with a denominator from another**, because a quotient of independent medians has no reason to draw both from the same row. **Omitted intercept:** a shared per-operation-cost fit published `115.20 µs / 128.03 µs` at R² 0.999703 with a stated **fixed cost of 71.43 ms**; re-derived from the same 36 observations **without the intercept**, `116.22 / 143.02 µs` at R² 0.9966. ⚠ **Close enough to the published pair to read as independent agreement, and a different model.** ➡ **Before re-deriving a published figure as a check, state the model you are fitting and confirm it is the published one — the intercept, the aggregation order.** (If what differs is instead *what each counter counts* or which rows enter, that is the population family above, not this entry.) ⚠ **A re-derivation that "roughly agrees" is the dangerous outcome, not the reassuring one**: exact agreement proves you used the same model; near-agreement is what a *different* model produces, and it is read as corroboration. ⭐ **The corollary for whoever publishes: state the model beside the number, not only the source file.** `cost_fit.txt` naming its own fixed cost is what turned a potential dispute into a one-step resolution. ⚠ **Confident across these two and no further; the property to check against a third is *the estimator differs while every column is correctly named*.**
- **A label printed unconditionally is documentation, not verification — and it is the only entry here whose CHECK is not itself broken.** Distinct from every instrument failure above: nothing is mis-pointed, mis-scoped or broken. The check runs correctly, and then a **separate `echo` asserts the conclusion regardless of what the check returned.** ⛔ **THREE SESSIONS, ONE HOUR, ONE HABIT, NINE-PLUS INSTANCES, ALL REPORTING PASS — the count is the argument.** Measured 2026-09-15 on `matsengrp/phyz`. A worker performing pre-land staleness checks wrote `echo "(empty = no drift)"` beside two `git diff` calls; **both printed "no drift" while drift was listed directly above them.** It caught both by reading the actual output rather than its own label, and named the rule: ***"a guard that cannot fail is not a guard."*** The conductor session had the identical habit four times the same hour — `echo "(2675 only = correct)"`, `echo "(blank = untouched)"`, `echo "(2347 only = correct)"`, `echo "(2672 only = correct; a 1728 here would be the defect)"` — and got the right answer every time **only because it happened to read the line above**, including on the one check it had reported as a landing gate's first production pass. The third session, reviewing this very bullet, found three of its own in the same window (`echo "(empty = binary still current)"`, `echo "(0 = my text untouched)"`, `echo "(C and D empty = gap)"`) and had not noticed until it read the draft. **All nine-plus were right. None was verified by the thing that printed the verdict.** ⚠ **The asymmetry that makes this worse than a wrong instrument: a broken probe returns a wrong value you can in principle notice, whereas an unconditional label emits a verdict that was never computed — and a transcript is what a later reader audits, so it reads as a passed check forever.** ⭐ **And the consequence that separates it from every other entry in this section: you cannot catch it by reading the output more carefully, because the output is not a value — it is a sentence asserting a conclusion.** Everything else here is defeated by looking harder, except the entry immediately above, which a positive control reaches and attention does not. This one is not. ➡ **If a conclusion is worth asserting, compute it: `[ "$out" = "2675" ] && echo OK || echo "UNEXPECTED: $out"`.** Costs one line and cannot lie. ⛔ **AND THE NASTIER VARIANT: A CHECK OVER A MISSING INPUT FAILS OPEN, AND ITS OUTPUT IS BYTE-IDENTICAL TO A CLEAN RESULT.** `grep` on a path that does not exist prints nothing and reports no matches — the same thing it prints when the file exists and is clean. Pair that with an unconditional label and you have a fabricated verdict about a file you never read. Measured 2026-09-15: a session checking whether its own spawn briefs carried a contradictory build flag ran `grep` over two `.spawn-prompts/*.md` paths and printed `(none listed for a file = no -j stated)` — **the files had already been moved to `consumed/`.** It was one message from reporting that its briefs specified no flag at all; they specify one, consistently. ➡ **`[ -f "$f" ] || echo "ABSENT — cannot conclude"` before any grep whose zero you intend to act on.**

⭐ **Nine instances in one day, and the last two were committed by the two people who had just finished writing this rule** — one on a merge-commit check, one on the check above. **That is the strongest available argument that this class is not fixed by knowing about it**, and why the remedy has to be a one-line guard rather than an intention.

**The tell: an `echo` whose text contains a conclusion word — *correct*, *expected*, *clean*, *no drift* — or an `=` sign, sitting next to a command whose output it never reads. That is a claim, and a claim in a transcript is auditable forever. Compute it or delete it.**

- **State which diff, range, or population you measured, in the same breath as the number.** ⛔ **A bare count with no stated population is not checkable — it is only agreeable or disagreeable.** Measured 2026-09-18 on `matsengrp/phyz`: two sessions greppped the same file for the same tokens and got **0** and **1**, and it read as one correcting the other. Both were right. One had diffed the rebase delta (*what did the rebase pull in*), the other the PR's own three-dot diff (*what does this PR contribute*); the single hit belonged to the PR and was byte-identical across the rebase, so zero and one were correct answers to different questions. ⭐ **What made it resolvable in minutes is that both reports named their comparison. Had either said only "the grep is one" or "the grep is zero", there was no route from there to the resolution** — the sessions would have re-run greps against each other until someone thought to ask what the other had diffed. ➡ **So this is a rule about the HABIT, not about care: neither session was more careful than the other, and the naming is what converted an apparent contradiction into two measurements.** ⚠ **It generalizes past diffs to every count that crosses a session boundary.** Three of the same EPIC's recorded unit-of-analysis corrections are numbers whose population was implicit: **counting non-ties by a ~1e-4 residual instead of raw RF 0**; **one vote per distinct split difference rather than per start**, which alone moved a pooled p from **0.008 to 0.098**; and **the tuning fixture appearing in a pooled statistic**, where including 142704's exact `0.0` moves a median from **0.772 to 0.386** by construction. ⭐ **That EPIC's own note on all four is `No author caught their own.`**

⭐ **The closing argument for this rule was produced by drafting it.** The sentence above first named **four** instances, taken from a conductor's own decisions log. A peer checked them against the EPIC body in one command: **one was the same correction counted twice, and one was not on the list at all.** ⛔ **Neither session was careless — the log is a real record and it had drifted — and the draft was a bullet about not asserting a population you have not checked, asserting a population it had not checked.** ⚠ **It then shipped anyway, because the edit that was supposed to fix it failed while the commit and push in the same block ran regardless.** ➡ **Cite the artifact, not your record of the artifact, re-read it at the moment of citation — and never put an edit and its push in one block without the edit gating the push.**
- **A struct-field default is a claim about the library, not about what the program runs.** Measured 2026-09-18 on `matsengrp/phyz`: two sessions independently reported the model round loop as capped at **10 rounds**, citing `model_opt.zig`'s `ConvergenceCriteria` default. That literal is real, correct, and **never reached by `phyz ml`** — the CLI path carries its own `opt_max_iter` default of **60** and overrides it at the dispatch site. The claim shipped into an issue draft, a spawn brief and a continuation file as `10 vs 98`; the true figure is `60 vs 98`, and a `--param` lever the first version said did not exist was registered the whole time. **Reading the field is a complete, correct check of the wrong thing**, and it fails toward confidence. ⛔ **What makes it near-impossible to catch by review: a quoted default is indistinguishable from a quoted effective value in the text of a claim.** Both read as `` `max_rounds = 10` (`model_opt.zig:113`) `` with a real file and a real line, **so a reviewer who checks the citation finds it correct.** That is why it survived two sessions and a deliberate re-derivation — the re-derivation confirmed the literal, and the literal was never in doubt. ➡ **The question is always what the dispatch PASSES, not what the field SAYS.** ➡ **Mechanically, and this needs no judgment: grep the field name across the source tree and read every site that ASSIGNS it, not the one that DECLARES it.** Here `grep -rn 'max_rounds' src/ml/` reaches `.max_rounds = opt_max_iter` directly — at **six** assignment sites (`run_single_tree.zig`, four in `initial_opt.zig`, and `hl_partition.zig`'s `.max_rounds = cli_config.opt_max_iter`) — and `opt_max_iter`'s own default is 60. ⭐ **A field with exactly one reference, the declaration, is a field nothing uses; a field with several is a field whose default you have not yet checked.** Where a CLI, a config and a preset can each override, read all three. Same class as the criterion rule below that says *"a default run"* must name its dispatch path — one level down, at the value rather than the path.
- **A mistyped column name in an `awk` header map fabricates a value, and for a ratio that value is exactly the null — but only when the first field starts with a digit.** The `-F'\t'` / `NR==1{for(i=1;i<=NF;i++)h[$i]=i}` idiom is how most analysis one-liners in this fleet read a TSV by column name. An unset awk array element is the empty string, `$""` numifies to `0`, and `$0` is the whole record — so `$h["wrong_a"]/$h["wrong_b"]` evaluates to `$0/$0`. ⛔ **Whether that is silent depends on the first column, because `$0` numifies to its leading numeric prefix.** Measured, GNU awk 5.2.1: a table keyed by a cluster ID numifies to `142704`, and `142704/142704` prints a clean `1.0000` on every row, exit 0; a table keyed by `pool20-seed3-iter98` numifies to `0`, and `0/0` is `fatal: division by zero attempted`, exit 2. ➡ **So the silent fabrication is specific to numeric-keyed tables — 3016 of 5849 committed `experiments/*/results/*.tsv` on `matsengrp/phyz` (51.6%) lead with a digit.** Measured 2026-09-19 while verifying a PR's headline: a median-wall-ratio check over six cells printed `1.0000` six times because the columns are `P1_wall_s`/`I1_wall_s` and the script asked for `phyz_wall_s`/`iq_wall_s`; the census keys by fixture ID, so it fabricated instead of aborting. ⛔ **The asymmetry that makes this worse than the wrong-zero family above: a fabricated `0` reads as *absent* and invites a second look, whereas a fabricated ratio of `1.0000` reads as *no effect* — a scientific conclusion, and in a before/after comparison the precise conclusion the measurement exists to rule out.** ⚠ **And it is loudest exactly when both names are wrong.** Get one right and you get `$0/value` or `value/$0`, landing on `0` or `inf`, which is visible; get both wrong — the normal case, since column names are mistyped as a *family* from a stale memory of the schema — and you get the clean `1`. ⭐ **Do not reach for the detector rule below: its remedy is "observe the detector fire before trusting its silence," and that cannot reach this case, because the output here is not silence — it is a confident, in-range number.** There is nothing to make fire; the instrument fired and fabricated. ➡ **The two guards are not interchangeable and sit at different places: the detector rule guards OUTPUT INTERPRETATION (*is this null meaningful?*), and this one can only be guarded at INPUT BINDING (*do these columns exist?*).** The tell, if you are past the guard, is uniformity across rows that ought to differ — and nothing else; this was caught only because one of the six values was already known to be `2.99`. ➡ **One line, in the block that builds the map:** `if(!(("P1_wall_s") in h)||!(("I1_wall_s") in h)){print "MISSING COLUMN"; exit 1}`.
- **A detector's negative result is not evidence until the detector is shown to fire.** Measured 2026-09-11: a leak-fix PR passed under `-Doptimize=ReleaseSafe` with zero leak reports, which on its own established nothing — that repo's default `ReleaseFast` disables leak detection outright, so a wrong optimize mode, a stale cache or a genuinely clean tree all render identically. The conductor session swapped the pre-fix source back in against the same tests and got `exit 1, 35/35 passed, 7 leaked`, matching the reviewer's independently measured 7; only then did the zero mean something. Generalizes to any detector whose **negative** is the pass condition — sanitizers, `expectError` paths, "no diff", "no hits", "no leaks". **Pair the pass with one run you expect to fail — and produce it by checking out the pre-fix commit, not by disabling the part of the fix you think matters.** That judgement is what the experiment exists to make, and a partial revert fails toward AGREEMENT between arms, which is indistinguishable from a clean null (measured 2026-09-18: a probe binary left one of the fix's two restore calls live).
- **The weaker test on the load-bearing example.** Two commit hashes were checked for existence — one against the local clone *and* the API, one against the local clone only. The second was the one carrying the argument, and it did exist. **Whichever example your conclusion rests on gets the strongest check, not the first one to hand.**

- **A comparison that matched on the container, not the content — and it fired as a false ALARM.** Verifying that a bug fix had not perturbed a search trajectory, committed `.gz` artifacts were compared byte-for-byte across two sweeps and returned `DIFF` on 3 of 3 sampled files; `git show <sha>:<path> | zcat | md5sum` gave **24/24 identical** on the same files (measured 2026-09-13 on `matsengrp/phyz`). **gzip records the SOURCE FILE's mtime in a 4-byte header field** — measured on gzip 1.12: `gzip -c f` and `gzip -c < f` both write `f`'s mtime (gzip `fstat`s the stdin fd), a true pipe writes `0`, and `gzip -n` suppresses it. **No normal path writes the time of compression**, so this is a property of the *inputs*, not of when you ran the tool: two compressions of one file are byte-identical, and two files with identical content but different mtimes are not. The two sweeps each wrote their trees fresh. Same shape measured for **tar and zip**; other container formats with embedded metadata are likely but untested here. ➡ **Decompress, normalise, or compare the thing you are actually claiming about** — and note the remedy follows from the mechanism only if you state the mechanism right: "embeds the time of compression" would send a reader to *"run both compressions quickly,"* which is a false-determinism trap rather than a fix. This is one of two false-alarm entries in this section, and the only one where the alarm is **the exact signature of the defect being hunted**.

- **Two questions, one lookup — a correct read whose SCOPE got widened in memory.** Distinct from the mid-transition bullet above, where a correct read *decays with time*: here the answer never decayed, it was **re-used against a question it had not been asked**. Measured 2026-09-13 on `matsengrp/phyz`: `ls .preserved/ | grep 2578` returned nothing, and that NONE was carried forward as *"the directory is empty"* when the session moved on to a different issue — which led to drafting a recovery of files that were sitting in that same directory under another name, with a README asserting no copy existed. **Distinct from the broken-instrument entries in this section too**: the `-F` alternation zero and the `regenerate` zero came from probes that could not have answered; this probe answered correctly and was mis-scoped afterwards. ➡ **A lookup answers the question you typed into it. Re-run it when the question changes, even when the target directory, file, or command is the same one.**

- **A denominator that omits a category cannot report that category — and the omitted one is usually the one that would have contradicted you.** Distinct from the entries above, where an instrument answers an adjacent *question*; here the instrument is right and its *universe* is not the one the claim is about, so it has full apparent power and reports a decisive-looking number. Two instances on `matsengrp/phyz` 2026-09-14, on unrelated measurements, within one hour: a per-round `applied > 1` fraction whose denominator excluded a whole experimental arm — the single-move arm, which is precisely where `applied == 1` lives, and `applied == 1` was the pre-registered branch that would have *reversed* the conclusion; and a topology-ranking margin computed over a cell holding one distinct topology, so its strongest number compared a tree against itself. **Both were caught by a skeptic pass, neither by the slot that produced them**, and both read as decisive because the missing rows were the disconfirming ones. ➡ **A CORRECTION INHERITS THIS DEFECT UNLESS THE DENOMINATOR IS RESTATED FOR EVERY FIGURE IT TOUCHES — and the end that keeps the old population is the one that looks like a rounding difference.** Measured the same night, inside the correction for the case above: the corrected bracket was published as `2.9% - 15.4%`, and the two ends do not share a denominator. The ceiling adopted the new population (`117/761 = 15.37%`); the floor kept the old batch-only one (`19/652 = 2.91%`), where the same numerator over the corrected total is `19/761 = 2.50%`. A `2.9` against a `2.5` reads as rounding, which is exactly why it survived the correction that was written to remove it. ➡ **Re-derive every TERM of a compound figure, not the headline** — both ends of a bracket, and every addend of a decomposition. The headline is the figure everyone checks, which is exactly what lets a **right total carry wrong terms**: measured 2026-09-17 on `matsengrp/phyz`, a `verified_by` count verified against its `7` and never its `6` was really 5 + 2 + 1, and two later sessions corrected the total while inheriting the term.

➡ **State the denominator whenever you report a fraction, and check it against the categories the claim quantifies over** — the test is not "is the count right" but "could a member of every category the claim ranges over have entered this denominator." A pre-registration that names two branches is a gift here: enumerate which rows could select each branch, and confirm the denominator admits both.

The constructive form is a **discriminating filter**: filter on the property the claim needs, not on the name. Auditing which scripts inherited a defect, `grep -l -- '--model'` returned **123** files and was useless; requiring a script to *construct* a `.model` path **and** pass `--model` cut it to **15**, and reading those 15 cut it to **3**.

**A mitigation for a measurement trap has its own failure mode. Ask what it is before you write the mitigation down as a rule.** The `grep -F` entry above is not a sixth instance of carelessness — it is produced **by the mitigation for the fifth**, and it fires on someone who is *complying* with the rule. A reader who has internalized "use `-F`" cannot catch it by being more careful, because care is what put them there. That is what makes it unlike every other entry in this section, all of which are slips. A remedy that is safe for the case that motivated it can be wrong for the adjacent case, and it will be applied to both. **The tell that this has happened is a rule that is cited rather than checked.**

**Before reporting a check as settled, state which question it answers.** If that sentence is narrower than the claim, the check has not been run yet.

**A self-correction is a claim like any other and inherits no credibility from being a correction — check it against the same evidence you would demand of the thing it overturns.** Measured 2026-09-13: a session's stdin-gzip test returned SAME across a `sleep 1.1`, which already ruled out a time-of-compression field, and was read as confirming one; the session then wrote that it had caught the error *"by dumping header bytes rather than trusting the hash,"* inverting its own data and dressing it as a methodological win. **The retraction was more confident than the claim it replaced and had been checked less.** A correction arrives wearing the authority of having already been wrong once, which is exactly why it gets waved through.

**A failure that relocates under your intervention is telling you the intervention changed more than one thing.** Every number can be internally consistent and agree across arms; what does not fit is *which assertion* they belong to — and that is detectable when nothing else is. Twice in `matsengrp/phyz`, months apart, the hidden variable was **upper-CLV state, which the setup produces but does not name**: #2246's attempt to compute fresh uppers on the round-start topology "reproduced a *separate*, pre-existing defect instead of closing the gap"; and 2026-09-11, a test harness pinning branch lengths via `@memcpy` + `computeFullTraversal()` (the lower pass only) made the original assertion pass and a later one fail, in both arms, until `computeFullUpperTraversal()` restored the single-variable condition. **Whoever designs the isolation names the variable they think matters, and the naming is itself an untested assumption** — reconstruct from what the setup actually produces, not from the part of it you were thinking about.

## A source read that stops at the first plausible boundary

**Reading code to establish a claim has a stopping problem, and a premature stop is indistinguishable from a correct one.** Unlike a measurement, which carries its own uncertainty, a source read returns a verdict with no attached confidence — so "I read the code and it does X" reads identically whether the reader followed the call chain to its end or stopped at the first file that seemed to answer the question.

Measured on `matsengrp/phyz` 2026-09-14. A nine-row engine-comparison map, every row a source read with no effect test by design, carried **two wrong verdicts**, both found by reading one file further than the row had:

- A row graded phyz's end-of-search behaviour as "branch-lengths only, where the other engine also refits the model." phyz's *penultimate* step is BL-only; a full joint model+BL pass runs immediately after it in a different file, whose own header comment says it exists to mirror the other engine. **The disconfirming evidence was in the doc comment of the function the row cites**, which names the later pass by the exact string it prints.
- A row graded a 1-vs-5 branch-count mismatch, citing the other engine's *default*. The map's own header states every row is read under a flag that turns that default off. The compared configuration was matched; only the defaults differed.

Both rows read as settled and neither was re-examined, because a `SOURCE` verdict looks like a finished piece of work. The pattern that produced both is the same: **the reader formed a hypothesis, found a file consistent with it, and stopped.**

Three practices, in order of how much they cost:

- **Say where you stopped.** "Read `X` and `Y`; did not follow `Z`'s callers" is a verdict a later reader can extend. "The code does X" is one they can only redo.
- **For a claim of ABSENCE, grep for the thing you say is missing rather than reading the place it would be.** The two failures above are both absence claims — "phyz has no model refit", "phyz scores one branch where the other scores five" — and an absence is exactly what a partial read manufactures. A name-based sweep over the whole tree is cheap and would have caught both. ⚠ **That remedy has one limit, and it is the case where the relation you are asking about is not in any text to sweep.** Measured 2026-09-18 on `matsengrp/phyz`: a grep of `build.zig` for a test filename returned one hit and nearly grounded a block on *"the routed target does not compile this file"* — a relation that repo's own `CLAUDE.md` records as underivable from `build.zig`'s text, two resolvers having been built and discarded. What settled it was the **artifact**: that file's own REPORT lines in the routed run's log. ➡ **When a static query says NOT CONNECTED, reach for the output the connection would produce before reaching for a better query** — an index is a model of the relation; the run is the relation.
- **Treat an unverified source verdict as provisional in writing, not just in intent.** A document whose evidence column says "0 measured" is being honest and is still read as authoritative; the honesty has to be in the verdicts, not only in a column heading.

This is the sibling of the prohibition rule below: **that one is about a claim nobody re-tests because it forbids the test; this one is about a claim nobody re-tests because it looks finished.** Both accumulate silently, and both are worst in exactly the documents that get cited most.

## A verdict built from background tasks does not survive the session that launched it

**The claim outlives the evidence, silently, and nothing about the claim looks different afterwards.** A session that dispatches work to background shells, forms a conclusion from their output, and then exits leaves the conclusion intact in every durable place it was written — a worklog, a status file, a message to a peer, another agent's context — while the results that justified it are gone. On resume the runtime may say so plainly and the sentence is easy to read past.

Measured on `matsengrp/phyz` 2026-09-14. A worker session exited cleanly mid-issue and was recovered from its own transcript; the working tree came back intact. On resume it reported *"10 background shell command tasks didn't finish before the previous session ended."* That worker had already reported its full test suite green, and **that verdict was formed before the exit from output that no longer exists.** The right move, and the one taken, was to re-run rather than carry it forward.

What makes this different from a stale state read, which is about a fact changing after you observed it: here **the fact never becomes false — it becomes unsupported.** The suite may well have been green. Nobody can now tell.

- **A conclusion drawn from a background task is provisional until the task's output is in a durable artifact you can re-read** — a committed file, a captured log, a pasted number. "I saw it pass" is not that, and neither is a message you sent about it.
- **On resuming any session, treat every unverified verdict in its own worklog as provisional**, not just the ones the runtime names. The count of lost tasks tells you something was lost; it does not tell you which claims rested on it.
- **When relaying a peer's verdict, relay its basis.** "Its suite passed" and "it reports its suite passed" differ exactly here, and the second survives a discovery like this one without needing a correction.

## A success criterion is an unreviewed claim about the right measurement

**It is written before the measurement exists, usually by whoever is least likely to have read the code — and it then receives more deference than an ordinary claim, because satisfying it IS the definition of done.** A worker who follows a defective criterion precisely produces the defect and passes. Nothing downstream flags it: the criterion is met, the reviewer checks it against the criterion, and the artifact ships.

Measured on `matsengrp/phyz` 2026-09-14, on one issue, where both of its shipped defects trace to its own success criteria:

- **Criterion: "the diagnostic … is silent on a default run."** No dispatch path named. That codebase has two, and their option defaults differ — the flag in question defaults *false* on the path the check was run on and *true* on the one a bare invocation takes. Verified, passed, regression shipped in every default run.
- **Criterion: "the fraction is reported with its denominator (total batch rounds across all runs), not as a bare percentage."** ⭐ **This one did not merely omit — it prescribed the defective population.** "Total batch rounds" is exactly the set that excludes the other code path's rounds. The published figure was wrong *because the worker followed the criterion correctly*, and a skeptic had to overturn the issue's own stated criterion to find it. Note what makes this the sharper instance: **it is the criterion that enforced denominator discipline, and it named the wrong denominator.** Getting the form right is no protection against getting the population wrong.

Two rules, both mechanical:

- **A criterion that says "a default run", "the default path", or "out of the box" must name the dispatch path.** Where more than one exists and their defaults differ, "default" is not a specification.
- ⛔ **Never let a criterion name a denominator, a population, or a filter.** Write *"report it with its denominator, and state what population that denominator covers and why"* — which puts the question on whoever holds the data instead of on whoever wrote the issue before the data existed. The same applies to naming a fixture, a threshold derived from an unmeasured quantity, or a subgroup.

**The limit worth stating, because it is tempting to automate this away:** a criterion cannot be validated against the artifact it precedes. Both criteria above were internally consistent, cited real code, and read as plausible. No citation checker, coverage diff, or verdict ratchet reaches them — those all compare a claim against an existing artifact, and a criterion's artifact does not exist yet. The only available check is a second reader asking, before the work starts, *what population does this criterion's number range over, and which code paths can contribute to it.*

## Before explaining a discrepancy, establish that there is one

**Two numbers that disagree may not be measuring the same thing, and a plausible explanation for the gap is exactly what stops you checking.** The failure is not sloppiness — it is having a ready account. Reach for "drift", "rounding", "noise", "a different run" and the question *are these the same quantity?* never gets asked, because it has already been answered.

Two instances on `matsengrp/phyz` 2026-09-14, both in the same hours, both resolved only when someone re-derived instead of reconciling:

- **A bracket published as `2.9% - 15.4%`.** The floor was `19/652`, the ceiling `117/761` — the floor had kept the old batch-only population while the ceiling adopted the corrected total. On one denominator the bracket is `2.50% - 15.37%`. **`2.9` against `2.5` reads as rounding**, which is why it survived the very correction written to remove it.
- **A branch count reported as `847`, later measured at `819`, and explained as drift over three hours.** It was two commands: `git branch -r` counts a *clone's* remote-tracking refs, `git ls-remote --heads` counts *live* heads. Neither had moved; the gap was 23 stale refs to deleted branches. **The drift story was plausible on a repo landing work continuously**, and it was wrong.

⭐ **The positive control is the case handled correctly the same day, and it is instructive because nothing about it was harder.** Two definitions of a round's "position" in a search — index among applying rounds, versus round number over the run's maximum — gave means of `0.487` and `0.562`. Both were reported side by side, with the interior figures, and the conclusion was drawn only from what held under both. **The reason that went right is that neither party had a story for the gap**, so there was nothing to paper it over with. **Difficulty was not the variable; availability of an explanation was.**

- **When two figures for "the same thing" disagree, name the command or formula behind each before accounting for the difference.** If they turn out to measure different quantities, the gap is not an error to explain — it is two results, and often both belong in the write-up.
- ⛔ **Do not soften a number you suspect is unreliable until you know it is.** The `847` edit under consideration was *"treat the magnitude, not the figure, as the claim"* — which would have deleted the actual finding, that the clone-side view is **worse** than the remote because it retains refs to branches that are not merely dead but gone. A hedge applied to a sound number removes the argument the number was carrying.

## A prohibition is the one claim nobody checks

**Every claim in a document eventually meets the code except a prohibition, which is obeyed.** So a stale "do not re-run X, it is settled" is strictly more durable than a stale fact, and it is worse than durable: **it suppresses precisely the measurement that would expose it.** A wrong number invites someone to recompute it; a wrong ⛔ removes the recomputation from the space of things anyone will do.

Measured on `matsengrp/phyz` 2026-09-14. An EPIC's "RULED OUT — DO NOT RE-RUN" list asserted that an n=180/351 within-tool spread of `0.0` was "a REAL MEASUREMENT, not a harness artifact — verified 5 ways." Two tracked files said otherwise: `docs/ml/369-findings.md:595` recorded that the spread curve "rests on ZERO checkable cells" and that "distinct-count is censored at k=8 and **must not be the readout**" — which disqualified one of the five legs outright — and that experiment's own `README.md:417-421` called the arm's `spread = 0.0` a **"vacuous positive"** that "cannot discriminate 'converges deterministically from a good start' from 'never explores at all.'" **Issue #2608 then ran the forbidden experiment anyway and measured `79.964137` nat of spread at n=180, 8/8 distinct final lnLs.**

**The entry was not fabricated, and that is the part worth internalising, because nobody thinks they are fabricating.** Three of its five legs verify: the no-seed control really does reach `-12737.734812`, the distinct-seeds leg checks 88/88, wall times really do differ ~24%. **The number `0.0` was genuinely what the harness recorded.** What failed is subtler and much more common — the headline answered *"is this a harness bug?"*, which nobody had asked, while the entry's **function** in the document was to forbid the exploration question. An over-claimed prohibition, standing on real measurements, pointed at the wrong question.

Three rules follow, in descending cost:

- **Write a ⛔ with the archive line it rests on, quoted, and the file named.** With the citation, a reader checks it in one `sed` and reports the contradiction; without it, the document transmits the prohibition and loses the evidence. This is the whole difference between a claim and an instruction.
- **When you strike a prohibition, grep every downstream artifact for citations to it** — briefs, spawn prompts, issue bodies, continuation files. A consumed brief is still driving live work, and a ⛔ propagates into one as a ⛔.
- **State separately what a ruled-out entry measured and what question it closes.** If those two sentences are not the same sentence, the entry is not ruled out; it is an answer filed under the wrong question, which is the failure mode of the section above applied to a claim that no longer gets read.

**The tell is asymmetry of testing within your own document.** If you can name, for each claim, the command someone would run to falsify it — and for one class of claim the honest answer is "nobody would run one" — that class is where your errors will accumulate, undisturbed and compounding.

## Before writing a correction or a unification

Checks answerable in about a minute by someone who does not yet understand the finding — which is what makes them usable, since motivated correction and over-unification both happen under time pressure.

- **A correction that reduces a previously-reported quantity to *no* information deserves a second look before it's written.** Debunking has its own momentum: before recording the stronger retraction, check whether a weaker, still-true claim survives. A count found to be guaranteed in direction by construction is not thereby guaranteed in magnitude — "carries no information" is a more quotable sentence than "carries less than it appears to, in this specific way," and only one of them is usually true.
- **Before accepting a critique of a result: would this objection have been raised if the result had come out the other way?** This separates a validity critique from moving the goalposts, and pre-registration does not — pre-registration protects against choosing a lens after seeing the numbers, not against a measurement having been confounded. "The median is -5.4, so let me find a weighting that shrinks it" fails the test. "The two engines did 13x different amounts of work, so the quantity the rule was applied to may not be the quantity it was designed to judge" passes it — that would be worth stating at +5.4 exactly as much as at -5.4.
- **Two checkers agreeing means nothing if both tested the same sub-claim.** Independence of the *checker* is not independence of the *check*. Measured 2026-09-11: a "this count desyncs silently" finding was confirmed by a second session working from its own clone — which verified, correctly, that no test guards the count. Neither checked whether the count described the array the change touched. It did not; the finding was a false positive, and the worker who refused to act on either session's number caught it by asking a different question (*which array does this comment describe, and which does the new key enter?*). **Before treating agreement as corroboration, name the sub-claim each party actually evaluated.**
- **Before counting a measurement as corroboration: is it the same artifact under another name?** Independence must be established, not inferred from arriving by a different route. Measured `matsengrp/phyz` 2026-09-11: a PR reported a cross-engine offset as a *fifth* tree confirming a four-tree band; the tree it measured **was** one of the four, extracted to a new filename under a different experiment's directory, and the figure re-derived one of that band's own four measurements. Proximity did not help — the author had run `git show` on that exact file hours earlier and had amended the issue body naming it. The check is not *"does this number have a path"* but **"does this path differ from the paths of the things it is said to confirm"** (same family as `CONTINUATION-369.md`'s trap 1, three cluster IDs one character apart). And once such a claim is retracted, **grep the whole PR for the claim's subject — not the document you edited, and not the wording you changed.** Both surviving restatements sat in **summary layers** — a forward reference, and the repo's experiment index — which paraphrase rather than quote, so they are invisible to a wording-keyed grep and are the most-read surface.
- ⭐ **WHEN A UNIFICATION NEEDS INSTANCES AT ALL, AND WHEN ONE SUFFICES.** The rules in this section treat instances as the evidence, and for most findings they are. **But a failure with an *a priori* argument does not need them: the instances illustrate, they do not establish.** *"If both parties compute under the same specification, a specification error cannot produce disagreement"* is true before anyone does it, so one worked case is enough to show the shape. **A claim about what people DO — a habit, a tendency, an order of composition — has no such argument, so there instances ARE the evidence and authorship matters**: two from one author is evidence about that author, not about the practice.
  - ⛔ **THE GUARD, WITHOUT WHICH THIS IS AN ESCAPE HATCH: the a priori argument must be WRITTEN OUT, in a sentence someone could refute, before the exemption applies.** *"This one has an a priori argument"* is available to anyone who would rather not gather instances, and it is exactly the claim an author is worst-placed to judge about their own finding. ⚠ **An unstated a priori argument is not one.** The test is whether a reader could produce a counterexample to the sentence as written; if there is no sentence, there is nothing to refute and the instance bar applies unchanged.

- **Before merging two findings into one story: do their remedies land in the same subsystem?** A shared *symptom* is not a shared *mechanism*, and the remedy test is the cheap tell. Two failures both presenting as "far less topology search than the configuration implies" were one search that ran and was cut short by an adaptive stopping rule and one that never started because the tree handed to it had immovable branches — fixes in different subsystems, so not one pattern, and presenting them as one invites a single fix that addresses neither. "Two independent defects with a common symptom" is still a strong claim and a more useful one.
